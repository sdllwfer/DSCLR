% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
\begin{abstract}
Retrieval requests often combine a target topic with positive criteria,
background context, and explicit exclusions.  Frozen retrievers rely heavily on
lexical and semantic entity matching, overlooking how these instructions
define relevance.  This failure is acute for exclusions, where an excluded
entity can become a strong matching signal.  Existing remedies often require
instruction training or representation adaptation.  We introduce \method{}, a
training-free reranking framework for frozen retrievers.  \method{} preserves
the complete request for retrieval and decomposes it into affirmative and
exclusion views, focusing positive evidence and removing irrelevant background
context.  The two views can still share topic information, causing raw
exclusion scores to penalize relevant content.  \method{} removes the exclusion
similarity predicted by affirmative relevance, then uses the residual to reduce
positive scores and suppress exclusion matches.  It requires no additional
training data, model updates, or document re-encoding.  Across three benchmarks
and four frozen retrievers, \method{} improves retrieval
effectiveness and instruction sensitivity, increasing the latter by up to 15.6
points and outperforming the strongest reported training-free baseline on
retrieval with exclusion constraints by up to 3.3 points.
\end{abstract}

% 1. INTRODUCTION
% ============================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Retrieval requests often combine a target topic with positive relevance
criteria, background context, and explicit exclusions.  Frozen retrievers rely
mainly on lexical and semantic matching, but these signals do not reliably
capture how each part of an instruction affects relevance.  The problem is
especially clear for exclusions: an excluded entity still appears in the
request and can become a strong matching signal.  Consider a request for
evidence that a US college education improves employment or income while
excluding documents about Manhattan colleges such as Columbia University.
Documents about employment outcomes for Columbia graduates match the topic and
entities in the request, yet they violate the exclusion.  This behavior agrees
with evidence that neural rankers are often insensitive to meaning changes
introduced by negation
\cite{DBLP:conf/eacl/WellerLD24,weller-etal-2025-followir}.

Separating the instruction into positive and exclusion views is a natural way
to make these roles explicit.  This decomposition also exposes a second
problem: the two views can still share topic information.  A relevant document
about a college outside Manhattan, for example, may score highly against the
exclusion ``Manhattan colleges such as Columbia University'' because both
concern US colleges and graduate outcomes.  The exclusion score then reflects
both shared topic information and evidence specific to the excluded content.
Penalizing the complete score can therefore suppress documents that satisfy the
request.

Existing work introduces instruction control through learned representations,
inference-time query adaptation, or explicit constraint reasoning
\cite{weller2024promptriever,dualview2026,gao2026instemb,deo2026,
xu2025logical,hoveyda2026orlog}.  Task-aware and instruction-tuned retrievers
show that natural-language descriptions can condition retrieval across tasks
and domains \cite{DBLP:conf/acl/AsaiSL0I0HY23,
DBLP:conf/acl/SuSKWHOYSZ023,DBLP:conf/iclr/CaiT0XGL0J23}.  These approaches
typically adapt the representation space or introduce an additional reasoning
procedure.  A complementary question is whether the scores of a frozen
retriever can be corrected directly, without retraining the model or rebuilding
the document index.  Such a correction must retain affirmative relevance while
distinguishing exclusion evidence from shared topical similarity.

We propose \method, a training-free reranking framework for this setting.
\method retains the complete instruction for initial retrieval, derives an
affirmative view that focuses the positive criteria and removes irrelevant
background context, and represents excluded content in a separate view.  For
each request, it estimates the component of the exclusion score predicted by
the affirmative score and uses the standardized residual as exclusion
evidence.  The final scoring rule preserves affirmative support when this
evidence is weak, then reduces that support and applies an exclusion penalty as
the residual increases.  This design operates on the scores of a frozen
retriever and requires no document re-encoding.

Across FollowIR, NegConstraint, and ExcluIR, \method improves instruction-aware
ranking across sparse and dense frozen retrievers.  It raises instruction
sensitivity by up to 15.6 points and exceeds the strongest reported
training-free reference on explicit-exclusion retrieval by up to 3.3 points.
Our contributions are threefold: (i) we introduce a training-free IF-IR
framework that uses full, affirmative, and exclusion views to handle positive
criteria, background context, and exclusions; (ii) we introduce residual
correction to separate exclusion-specific evidence from shared topic
information, together with a scoring rule that combines relevance promotion
and exclusion suppression; and (iii) we provide controlled evidence across
three benchmarks and multiple retriever families, separating the effects of
evidence construction, residualization, and score composition.

% 2. RELATED WORK

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Learning Instruction-Following Retrievers}

Instruction-following behavior can be learned through retriever adaptation.
Early task-aware retrieval systems condition relevance on natural-language
task descriptions: TART uses multitask instruction tuning, INSTRUCTOR learns a
shared instruction-conditioned embedding space, and HypeR composes
task-dependent prompts for cross-domain retrieval
\cite{DBLP:conf/acl/AsaiSL0I0HY23,DBLP:conf/acl/SuSKWHOYSZ023,
DBLP:conf/iclr/CaiT0XGL0J23}.
FollowIR-7B, Promptriever, and InF-IR train retrievers with natural-language
relevance instructions, while Dual-View Training adds polarity-reversed
supervision
\cite{weller-etal-2025-followir,weller2024promptriever,zhuang2025infir,
dualview2026}.  InstEmb instead combines input-intrinsic and output-aware
semantic signals when learning instruction-following embeddings
\cite{gao2026instemb}.  In each case, instruction sensitivity is encoded by the
training data and objective.  \method{} keeps the representation function fixed
and introduces instruction control at the ranking stage.

\subsection{Inference-Time Query Adaptation}

Inference-time methods expose structure that a monolithic query may obscure.
Query rewriting and expansion reformulate the request to improve relevance
matching \cite{gao2023hyde,wang2023query2doc,raudaschl2024ragfusion,
li2025deepretrieval}.  Stage-Aware Decomposition retains the complete query for
initial retrieval and additively aggregates decomposed condition scores during
reranking, treating each condition as positive matching evidence
\cite{yin2026stageaware}.  DEO instead derives positive and negative subqueries
and optimizes the query embedding toward the former and away from the latter
\cite{deo2026}.  \method{} also decomposes the request, but neither replaces nor
optimizes its representation.  It preserves the complete, affirmative, and
exclusion queries as separate scoring views and resolves their evidence after
the documents have been scored.

\subsection{Constraint-Aware Ranking and Reasoning}

Negation has long been a difficult case for learned representations.  Targeted
augmentation can improve negation sensitivity in pretrained language models
\cite{DBLP:conf/emnlp/SinghKS23}, while NevIR shows that many neural rankers
struggle to distinguish otherwise similar documents whose meanings differ
through negation \cite{DBLP:conf/eacl/WellerLD24}.  These studies motivate
negation-aware modeling, but explicit exclusion retrieval additionally requires
balancing a violation signal against the topical relevance shared by desired
and excluded content.

Explicit constraints can be handled through score correction or structured
reasoning.  In vision--language retrieval, SpaceVLM-DRC constructs multiple
query directions and adds a repulsion penalty based on raw negated-concept
similarity \cite{pokhrel2026negation}.  Directly penalizing raw exclusion
similarity is unreliable in text retrieval when desired and excluded content
share entities or topical context: a relevant document can score highly
against both.  \method{} instead leaves query and document representations
unchanged, removes the component of the exclusion score predicted by
affirmative relevance, and uses the remaining residual for reranking.

Structured approaches enforce constraints at a different inference level.
NS-IR maps queries and documents into logical forms and enforces connective
consistency for negative-constraint retrieval \cite{xu2025logical}, whereas
OrLog decomposes complex requests into predicates and applies probabilistic
reasoning \cite{hoveyda2026orlog}.  These methods target predicate-level
constraint satisfaction, whereas \method{} provides score-level correction
for frozen retrievers.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\method has three steps.  It retrieves candidates with the complete
instruction, scores them under affirmative and exclusion views, and corrects
their ranks using residual exclusion evidence.  The following subsections
define these evidence views, the residual exclusion score, and the asymmetric
scoring rule.

\centering

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Evidence Views}

\method receives a natural-language request $u$ containing the retrieval topic
and its instructions.  The frozen retriever uses the complete request as the
full query $\qbase=u$ and returns the top-$K$ candidate set
$\mathcal C_u$.  An LLM decomposes $u$ once into two auxiliary queries:
$\qpos$ states the content that supports relevance, and $\qneg$ states the
content to exclude.  If the request contains no exclusion, the decomposer
returns $\qneg=\textnormal{[NONE]}$.  The candidate set is always retrieved
with $\qbase$; the auxiliary views are used only for reranking.

The three views serve different roles.  The full query retains the complete
topic, context, and instruction during candidate generation.  The affirmative
view removes the exclusion clause and non-criterion context to provide a
focused relevance signal.  The exclusion view states the rejected content as a
positive description, allowing the retriever to score whether a document
matches that content.  In addition to supporting relevance, the affirmative
score provides a reference for the topical component shared with the exclusion
score.  All views use the same frozen scorer.

Let $s(Q,d)$ denote the score assigned to document $d$ under query $Q$.  For
each $d\in\mathcal C_u$, the three channels are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \sbase(d) &= s(\qbase, d), \\
 \spos(d)  &= s(\qpos, d), \\
 \sneg(d)  &= s(\qneg, d).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:trix-channels}
\end{equation}
For a dense retriever, $s(Q_x,d)=\cos(f_q(Q_x),f_d(d))$ is the embedding
similarity; for BM25, it is the lexical retrieval score.  Dense document
embeddings are computed once and reused across views, while BM25 uses one
fixed index.

affirmative view retains the desired content, and the exclusion view converts
the rejected content into a directly scorable description.

\subsection{Residual Exclusion Score}

The raw exclusion score mixes exclusion-specific evidence with topical
similarity shared by the affirmative and exclusion views.  For example, when
the desired topic is genetically engineered food and the exclusion is the
Flavr Savr tomato, a relevant document about genetically engineered soybeans
can score highly under both views because both concern the broader topic.
Directly subtracting the exclusion score penalizes this shared topical
component together with evidence specific to the excluded content.

\method separates the two components by measuring whether a document's
exclusion score is unusually high given its affirmative score.  For each
request, it fits an affine relation between the normalized affirmative and
exclusion scores over $\mathcal C_u$.  The standardized signed deviation from
this relation defines the residual exclusion score.  Fitting the relation per
request adapts the reference to the score geometry induced by each instruction.

\method first robustly standardizes all three score channels over the retrieved
set $\mathcal C_u$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 z_x(d)&=\frac{S_x(d)-\operatorname{median}_{d'\in\mathcal C_u}S_x(d')}
 {\operatorname{MAD}_{d'\in\mathcal C_u}(S_x(d'))+\epsilon},\\
 &\qquad x\in\{\mathrm{full},\mathrm{pos},\mathrm{neg}\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:trix-normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{MAD}(S)=\operatorname{median}|S-\operatorname{median}(S)|$
and $\epsilon>0$ prevents division by zero.  This normalization aligns
query-specific offsets and scales while limiting the influence of extreme
scores \cite{rousseeuw1993mad}.  \method then fits the affine relation with
Huber regression:
\begin{equation}
 (\hat a_u,\hat b_u)=\arg\min_{a,b}\sum_{d\in\mathcal C_u}
 \rho_\delta\!\left(z_{\mathrm{neg}}(d)-a-bz_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)\right),
 \label{eq:trix-huber}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\delta$ is the Huber loss with transition point $\delta$.  The fit
captures the dominant score association, while the Huber loss limits the
influence of large deviations \cite{huber1964robust}.  It provides a
query-specific score-space reference for exclusion similarity.
For $e(d)=z_{\mathrm{neg}}(d)-\hat a_u-\hat b_u z_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)$,
we robustly standardize the residual:
\begin{equation}
 r(d)=\frac{e(d)-\operatorname{median}_{d'\in\mathcal C_u}e(d')}
 {\operatorname{MAD}_{d'\in\mathcal C_u}(e(d'))+\epsilon}.
 \label{eq:trix-residual}
\end{equation}
The second standardization makes residual magnitudes comparable across
requests: $r(d)=0$ is the median fitted deviation, and one unit is measured
relative to the within-request residual dispersion.  This common scale allows
the same decision parameters to be used across queries and retrievers.
Positive $r(d)$ indicates more exclusion similarity than predicted by the
document's affirmative score; negative $r(d)$ indicates less.  The residual is
a relative score-space signal, not a calibrated probability of constraint
violation.  Numerical details are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\subsection{Residual-Guided Asymmetric Scoring}

\method uses the residual signal to regulate affirmative support and suppress
strong exclusion matches.  We first retain the positive part of the normalized
affirmative score:
\begin{equation}
 p(d)=[z_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)]_+.
 \label{eq:trix-positive}
\end{equation}
Because $z_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)$ is median-centered, $p(d)$ rewards only documents
whose affirmative score is above the candidate-set median.  We activate the
exclusion correction beyond a shared boundary $\lambda\geq0$:
\begin{equation}
 h(d)=[r(d)-\lambda]_+,\qquad
 g(d)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{h(d)}{\tau}\right),\quad \tau>0,
 \label{eq:trix-attenuation}
\end{equation}
where $h(d)$ is the active exclusion evidence and $g(d)\in(0,1]$ is its
attenuation factor.  Below the boundary, $h(d)=0$ and $g(d)=1$; above it,
$h(d)$ grows linearly while $g(d)$ decreases smoothly.
The final score is
\begin{equation}
 \sfinal(d)=z_{\mathrm{full}}(d)+p(d)g(d)-h(d).
 \label{eq:trix-final}
\end{equation}
The term $p(d)g(d)$ retains the affirmative reward under weak exclusion
evidence and attenuates it once the boundary is crossed.  The term $-h(d)$
directly suppresses strong exclusion matches, so the correction can lower a
document below its full-query score.  For fixed $z_{\mathrm{full}}(d)$ and
$p(d)$, Eq.~\ref{eq:trix-final} is therefore non-increasing in $r(d)$.
When no exclusion view is present, \method sets $h(d)=0$ and $g(d)=1$, reducing
the score to $z_{\mathrm{full}}(d)+p(d)$.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:evaluation-overview}

\paragraph{Benchmarks and Metrics.}
FollowIR \cite{DBLP:conf/naacl/WellerCMLCDLS25} is our primary benchmark for
changed-instruction retrieval on Core17, Robust04, and News21.
We report MAP on Core17 and Robust04, nDCG on News21, and p-MRR
\cite{DBLP:conf/naacl/WellerCMLCDLS25} for sensitivity to instruction changes.
We interpret relevance and p-MRR jointly because either can improve at the
expense of the other.  We also test transfer on NegConstraint
\cite{xu2025logical}, using MAP and nDCG@10, and ExcluIR
\cite{DBLP:conf/aaai/ZhangZ0PRRCR25}, using MRR and Right Rank (RR).  RR is the
percentage of desired--excluded document pairs ordered correctly.  ExcluIR is
used only for evaluation.

\paragraph{Models and Evaluation Protocol.}
The frozen Qwen3-4B model \cite{yang2025qwen3} produces $\qpos$ and $\qneg$ in
one structured generation; $\qbase$ remains the original request and retrieves
the candidates.  FollowIR experiments use BM25
\cite{DBLP:journals/ftir/RobertsonZ09} together with the frozen E5-Mistral,
BGE-large, and RepLLaMA bi-encoders
\cite{DBLP:conf/acl/WangYHYMW24,DBLP:conf/sigir/XiaoLZMLN24,DBLP:conf/sigir/MaWYWL24}
and rerank each backbone's top-$K$ candidates, where
$K=\textnormal{TBD}$.  NegConstraint and ExcluIR use BGE-small-en-v1.5,
BGE-large-en-v1.5, and BGE-M3 \cite{DBLP:conf/sigir/XiaoLZMLN24}.
Within every matched comparison, variants share candidates, generated views,
retrieval templates, scorer configuration, and document representations.
The same $\lambda=\textnormal{TBD}$ and $\tau=\textnormal{TBD}$ are fixed
without test relevance labels and used across datasets and retrievers.
Prompt, decoding, normalization, and numerical details are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\paragraph{Baselines and Controlled Comparisons.}
On FollowIR, FollowIR-7B, Promptriever, and InstEmb
\cite{DBLP:conf/naacl/WellerCMLCDLS25,DBLP:conf/iclr/WellerDLPZH25,gao2026instemb}
provide instruction-trained system-level references.  We also reproduce
HyDE, Query2Doc, and ConvSearch-R1 with RepLLaMA
\cite{DBLP:conf/acl/GaoMLC23,DBLP:conf/emnlp/WangYW23,
DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2503-00223}.
Per-collection comparisons are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:followir-full}.  DEO
\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2603-09185} and the strongest results reported
with NegConstraint and ExcluIR provide system-level context.  We report these
separately from the matched Base/$+\method$ comparisons because their training
and retrieval pipelines differ.  Paired uncertainty for the central component
comparisons is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:statistical-reliability}.

\subsection{Mechanism Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism-analysis}

\paragraph{Residual Signal.}
Figure~\ref{fig:residual-mechanism} illustrates why raw exclusion similarity is
an imprecise suppression signal.  For this query,
$z_{\mathrm{neg}}$ rises with $z_{\mathrm{pos}}$, so direct subtraction also
penalizes the exclusion score predicted by affirmative relevance.  The fitted
Huber relation estimates this shared component, and the standardized deviation
$r$ retains candidates whose exclusion similarity is unusually high relative
to their affirmative score.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/figure2.pdf}
\caption{Residualization for an illustrative query.  Left: the Huber relation
between affirmative and exclusion scores.  Right: standardized deviations
after removing the fitted component.  Positive residuals identify candidates
with more exclusion similarity than predicted by their affirmative score.}
\label{fig:residual-mechanism}
\end{figure}

The raw-versus-residual comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation-residual} tests the ranking effect of this change; the
cross-benchmark comparison is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:exclusion-benchmarks}.

\paragraph{Effects on Ranking.}
\method converts the residual into a document-level score adjustment.  Its
effect relative to positive-view scoring and the full-query baseline is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \Delta_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)
 &=\sfinal(d)-\bigl(z_{\mathrm{full}}(d)+p(d)\bigr) \\
 &=p(d)\bigl(g(d)-1\bigr)-h(d),\\
 \Delta_{\mathrm{base}}(d)
 &=\sfinal(d)-z_{\mathrm{full}}(d)
 =p(d)g(d)-h(d).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:trix-adjustments}
\end{equation}
When $r(d)\leq\lambda$, $h(d)=0$ and $g(d)=1$, so
$\Delta_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)=0$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{base}}(d)=p(d)\geq0$:
the positive contribution is preserved.  Above the boundary,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{pos}}(d)<0$; the positive contribution is attenuated as the
exclusion penalty increases.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/dual_rank_scatter_followir_repllama_v8.pdf}
\caption{Base rank versus \method{} rank for documents from FollowIR
exclusion queries.  Each point is a document; diamonds denote documents
that remain relevant after the exclusion change, and circles denote
documents excluded by the new instruction.  Points above the diagonal
are promoted by \method; points below it are suppressed.}
\label{fig:reward-penalty-effect}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:reward-penalty-effect} shows the resulting rank changes.  The
documents that remain relevant after the instruction change tend to move above
the diagonal, while excluded documents tend to move below it.  The separation
is clearest among the highest-ranked documents, where changes have the largest
effect on retrieval metrics.  This behavior agrees with
Eq.~\ref{eq:trix-adjustments}: the affirmative term promotes relevant
documents, while attenuation and the explicit penalty suppress strong
exclusion matches.

\FloatBarrier
Overall, the experiments show that \method improves relevance and instruction
sensitivity across sparse and dense retrievers, transfers to two explicit-
exclusion benchmarks, and benefits from both residual signal construction and
asymmetric score composition.  Estimator alternatives, sensitivity, paired
uncertainty, and latency are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-ablations}.

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented \method{}, a training-free reranking method that
corrects the scores of a frozen retriever to better follow exclusion
instructions.  Across three benchmarks and four retriever families,
\method{} consistently improves instruction-sensitive ranking without
retraining or document re-encoding.

\method relies on two conditions: the LLM decomposition must expose useful
affirmative and exclusion views, and the initial retriever must provide
adequate candidate coverage.  Its local affine correction may be less
effective when these views remain tightly coupled in the retriever's score
space.  These conditions define the intended scope of score-level correction
and motivate future combinations with stronger decomposition and explicit
constraint reasoning.

\section{Decomposition and Reproducibility Details}
\label{app:implementation}

The instruction decomposer is the frozen Qwen3-4B model
\cite{yang2025qwen3}.  It receives the complete user request and returns
structured affirmative and exclusion content in one generation.  The full
view $\qbase$ is the original request itself, used unchanged for candidate
generation; $\qpos$ retains the topic and affirmative conditions while removing
exclusion content, and $\qneg$ contains the excluded content, or
\textnormal{[NONE]} when no exclusion is present.  We use the fixed
\texttt{TSC\_BALANCED\_t01} prompt with temperature $0.1$, top-$p=0.9$, at most
512 new tokens, and thinking disabled.  Malformed or missing fields are mapped
to the corresponding empty view.  The system prompt is:
{\scriptsize
\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert IR query optimizer for Dense
Vector Models. Decouple "Original Query" and
"Instruction" into Q_plus and Q_minus.

Q_plus rules: (1) Fluent natural language phrase,
not a keyword list. (2) Semantic topic only;
remove format noise ("articles", "documents",
"reports"). (3) Remove conversational connectors
("such as", "including"). (4) Sharp, coherent.

Q_minus rules: (1) Extract only critical
high-level exclusion concepts. (2) Concise
natural phrasing. (3) If no exclusion,
output "[NONE]".

No-negation rule: DENSE RETRIEVERS IGNORE LOGIC
WORDS. NEVER use negation in Q_minus ("no",
"not", "non-", "without"). Convert negations to
AFFIRMATIVE entities:
  BAD: "non-United Kingdom"
  -> GOOD: "United States, France, global"
  BAD: "violence outside Ireland"
  -> GOOD: "violence in England, international"
Output ONLY exact entities to exclude.

Output JSON:
{
  "Reasoning_Steps": "1. Core topic. 2. Filter
    noise. 3. Exclusions (negations->affirm).",
  "Q_plus": "Fluent natural language query",
  "Q_minus": "Core exclusions or [NONE]"
}
\end{verbatim}
}
The user prompt template is:
{\scriptsize
\begin{verbatim}
Decouple into Q_plus and Q_minus:
Query: "{query}"
Instruction: "{instruction}"
\end{verbatim}
}

All document representations are computed once with the base retriever's
document template, and all three query views use its query template.  For
numerical stability, Huber residuals and channel scores use a
$10^{-6}$ floor in their median/MAD normalization.  The regression is fit
independently on each candidate set and uses no relevance labels.  The
scoring hyperparameters $\lambda$ and $\tau$ are fixed before evaluation and
shared across datasets and retrievers; no query-specific tuned coefficients or
metric-based tuning are introduced.  The released configuration records
these constants and the candidate-set depth for each experiment.
